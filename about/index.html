Who I Am

A designer who finds the way through.

Every design challenge starts like a maze: no clear view of the way out, just walls in front of you. UX design is the practice of walking it first, so the people who come after don’t have to.

Hi, I’m Haopeng Liu, a UX Designer & Researcher based in Seattle. For the past 6+ years, I’ve been getting delightfully lost in ambiguity, on purpose, discovering where people hesitate, uncovering the patterns beneath the noise, and turning that into something that feels like a straight line.

That’s shown up as turning early-stage ideas into experiences people actually enjoy using, translating millions of data points into direction teams can act on, and simplifying complex, high-stakes enterprise systems into flows people trust without thinking twice. Right now, I’m especially drawn to the maze of AI-native design: building in the guardrails, trust, and moments of control that keep people oriented even as the tools get smarter.

How I work

Nine moves I return to whether I’m sketching alone or steering a room full of stakeholders. Scroll to walk them →

Define the goal01

I walk backward from the goal.

Before I sketch anything, I want to know what success looks like, for the user and for the business. Then I work backward from that point to find the most efficient path, rather than designing forward and hoping it eventually connects.

Find where to start02

I move toward the fog, not away from it.

The undefined parts of a project, the flows nobody has mapped out yet, are usually where I start. That's where the real risk lives, and the earlier it surfaces, the cheaper it is to solve.

Plan the roadmap03

I map before I build — but I don't map forever.

Understanding the shape of a problem, its constraints, edge cases, and limitations, matters more to me than jumping straight into screens. Once that shape is clear, though, further planning has diminishing returns. I'd rather test a real prototype and learn from it than refine a plan that hasn't been challenged yet.

Understand the structure04

I treat walls as clues, not enemies.

Technical limits, business requirements, and timelines aren't things to design around. They're what turn an unlimited set of options into a solvable problem. Before working around a constraint, I ask why it exists. Some are arbitrary and worth pushing back on. Others are essential to the system. Knowing the difference is part of the job.

Test and iterate05

I don't trust the first path.

The most obvious answer is often missing something: an edge case, a user behavior the happy path doesn't account for. So I pressure-test my own ideas early, looking for what will break before a user finds it for me.

Design with AI06

I use faster tools to walk more paths, not to skip the walking.

AI-assisted prototyping lets me test several directions in the time it used to take to test one. It doesn't replace judgment. I still decide which direction is worth pursuing. It simply gets me to that decision faster.

Make sense of failure07

I treat dead ends as data, not failure.

A concept that doesn't work isn't wasted effort. It narrows the problem and sharpens the next attempt. I'd rather rule out several weak ideas quickly than defend one mediocre idea for months.

Zoom out to patterns08

I look for the pattern, and leave a map behind.

When I encounter the same issue more than once, it's rarely a coincidence. It usually points to something systemic worth fixing at the source. So I document as I go, through specs, rationale, and clear handoffs, so the same problem doesn't have to be solved twice.

Land on the user09

I design for the person walking through it, not for myself.

Solving the problem is my job. Living with an unsolved one shouldn’t be the user’s. Every decision, from defaults to copy to the friction I choose to remove, works toward one outcome: the final experience should feel obvious, even if getting there wasn’t.

Working with AI

AI is my lantern, not my compass.

I use AI to widen the search space and move faster through the maze — never to decide which exit matters. Here’s how a typical loop runs.

1

Diverge

I prompt for a dozen framings and layouts at once, then throw most away. Volume kills the blank page.

2

Interrogate

I pressure-test each direction against real research — asking the model to argue against its own suggestion.

3

Prototype

Promising paths become clickable in hours, not weeks. I hand users something to break early.

4

Decide

This part stays human. I weigh the trade-offs, own the call, and keep the receipts.
